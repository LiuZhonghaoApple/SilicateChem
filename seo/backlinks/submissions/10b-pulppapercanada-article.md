## Sodium Silicates in Pulp Bleaching and Deinking: Function, Trade-offs and Control

Few additives in a mechanical-pulp or deinking line do as many jobs at once as sodium silicate. In a hydrogen-peroxide bleaching stage it behaves as a peroxide stabilizer, an alkaline buffer and a metal-ion scavenger — and in deinking it also supports ink detachment and keeps dislodged ink from settling back onto fibre. Because it works on several fronts, silicate is easy to over-dose and easy to blame when deposits appear downstream. This article reviews what sodium silicates actually do in bleaching and deinking, how to choose between grades, and how to keep the benefits while controlling the well-known drawbacks.

### Why peroxide needs help

Hydrogen peroxide bleaches lignin-rich pulps under alkaline conditions, typically around pH 10.5–11. The problem is that peroxide is unstable in exactly those conditions when transition-metal ions — chiefly manganese, iron and copper — are present. These metals catalyze the decomposition of peroxide into oxygen and water, wasting bleaching chemical and generating radicals that can attack the pulp and cap brightness. Mechanical pulps and recovered furnish both carry variable metal loads from wood, process water and recycled fibre, so uncontrolled decomposition is the default state, not the exception.

Two complementary strategies address this: remove or deactivate the metals, and stabilize the peroxide that remains. Sodium silicate contributes to both.

### What silicate does in the bleaching stage

**Metal sequestration.** Silicate forms colloidal species in alkaline liquor that adsorb and help deactivate transition-metal ions, reducing their catalytic effect on peroxide. It is not a true chelant, which is why silicate is usually paired with a chelant such as DTPA or EDTA applied in a pretreatment stage. The chelant removes the bulk of the free metals; the silicate buffers the system and mops up residual catalytic activity during bleaching.

**Alkaline buffering.** Peroxide bleaching has a relatively narrow effective pH window. Silicate provides buffering that helps hold the liquor in range as alkali is consumed, giving more stable brightness development than caustic alone.

**The magnesium synergy.** Silicate is frequently used with magnesium sulphate. Magnesium and silicate together are thought to form colloidal complexes that both protect peroxide and reduce alkaline attack on carbohydrates, preserving pulp strength. The silicate/Mg/chelant combination remains a workhorse of alkaline peroxide bleaching.

The net result of a well-tuned silicate program is higher peroxide efficiency — more brightness for the same charge, or the same brightness for less peroxide — with better run-to-run consistency.

### What silicate does in deinking

Deinking lines add a second set of demands. In the pulping and pre-flotation chemistry, alkaline silicate assists ink detachment by helping saponify oil- and resin-based inks and by raising and buffering pH. Just as important, silicate helps disperse detached ink particles and discourages their redeposition onto fibre, protecting brightness through the process. Where a peroxide bleaching stage is part of the deinking sequence, the same stabilizing role described above applies.

Silicate chemistry does interact with the rest of the deinking formulation. In flotation deinking that uses fatty-acid soap collectors and calcium, silicate influences the calcium balance and foam behaviour, so silicate dose cannot be optimized in isolation from surfactant and hardness settings.

### Choosing the silicate: ratio, form and purity

Not all sodium silicates behave the same, and the differences matter on the machine.

- **SiO₂:Na₂O weight ratio.** Liquid "water glass" grades commonly run near a 3.2 or 2.0 ratio; sodium metasilicate corresponds to a 1.0 ratio and is the most alkaline. A lower ratio delivers more alkalinity per unit of silicate, which can reduce separate caustic demand; a higher ratio delivers more silica for buffering and colloidal effects with less added alkali. The right choice depends on how much of the job you want the silicate to do versus your caustic and buffering strategy.
- **Liquid vs solid.** Liquid silicate is convenient to meter but is a dilute, freight-heavy product; solid grades (including metasilicate) are concentrated and simpler to store but must be dissolved correctly. The decision is usually driven by dosing infrastructure and logistics.
- **Iron content.** Because iron is itself a peroxide-decomposition catalyst, a silicate carrying significant iron works against the very stability it is added to provide. Consistent low-iron specification is a quiet but real quality lever, and one worth confirming on the certificate of analysis rather than assuming.

### The trade-off everyone knows: deposits

The standard objection to silicate is scaling. Silica and silicate-metal deposits can build on wires, felts, rolls, screens and in white-water and shower systems, and they can be stubborn to remove. Silicate also adds dissolved "dead load" that circulates in closed water systems.

None of this is a reason to abandon silicate; it is a reason to dose deliberately. Practical control measures include:

- **Chelant pretreatment** to lower the incoming metal load so less silicate is needed for the same peroxide protection.
- **Right-sizing the dose** to the metal and brightness target rather than to a historical set-point; excess silicate mostly becomes deposit and dead load.
- **Water-hardness management**, since calcium and magnesium interact with silicate and influence where deposits form.
- **Monitoring** residual peroxide and brightness so the silicate charge can be trimmed to the minimum that holds the result.

Some mills reduce or replace silicate with organic stabilizer packages to cut deposits; others find the cost and robustness of silicate hard to beat. Either way, the decision should be made on measured peroxide efficiency and deposit data, not on habit.

### Sourcing and handling

Sodium silicate is a strongly alkaline material and should be handled as such: appropriate PPE, compatible materials of construction, and controlled dissolution for solid grades to avoid caking and exotherm. From a procurement standpoint, the useful questions are consistency and documentation — a stable SiO₂:Na₂O ratio, controlled iron and insolubles, and a certificate of analysis and safety data sheet with each lot, so that bleaching and deinking chemistry does not shift because the additive did.

### Bottom line

Sodium silicate remains one of the most cost-effective multi-function additives in alkaline peroxide bleaching and deinking. It stabilizes peroxide, buffers the liquor, scavenges residual catalytic metals and, in deinking, helps detach and disperse ink. The engineering task is not whether to use it but how to use the least that delivers the target — pairing it with chelant pretreatment, matching grade and ratio to the job, specifying low iron, and monitoring so the dose stays honest. Done well, silicate quietly protects both brightness and the bleaching budget.
