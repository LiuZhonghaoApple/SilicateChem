## Qualifying an Inorganic Chemical Supplier: What Buyers Should Actually Verify

Commodity inorganic chemicals look like the easy part of a procurement portfolio. The specification is short, the chemistry is old, and there are many suppliers quoting. That apparent simplicity is exactly why qualification tends to be thin — and why the failures, when they come, arrive as an unexplained process deviation rather than a rejected delivery.

Using alkaline silicates as a worked example, this article sets out what is worth verifying before a supplier is approved, which documents actually evidence quality, and how to design incoming control around the failure modes that genuinely occur.

### First question: are you buying from a manufacturer or a trader?

Neither answer is disqualifying. A good trader with a stable source can outperform a marginal producer, and traders often provide useful flexibility on small lots. But the two require different qualification.

With a manufacturer you can audit the process that creates the variability you care about. With a trader you are qualifying a supply chain you cannot see, so the relevant questions change: which plant actually produces the material, does that stay constant, and what happens to your specification if they switch source mid-contract?

Practical checks that separate the two: ask for the production address rather than the office address; ask which process route is used; ask what happens to off-specification batches. A producer answers these fluently. A reseller may not know — which is important information, not a reason to walk away.

### Which documents evidence quality, and which are decorative

Buyers routinely collect a folder of certificates, then rely on the wrong ones.

**Genuinely useful:**

- **A technical data sheet** stating the specification as a range with test methods named. "Purity ≥ 99%" without a method is not a specification, it is marketing.
- **A batch-specific certificate of analysis** showing actual measured values for the lot you received, with lot number and date. A COA that shows identical round numbers on every batch is a template, not a measurement.
- **A safety data sheet** in the format required by your jurisdiction and consistent with the grade supplied.
- **Traceability**: the ability to link the lot number you received back to a production record. Test it once — ask about a lot you took six months ago and see what comes back.

**Weaker evidence than buyers assume:**

- A generic ISO 9001 certificate proves a quality system exists somewhere in the organisation; it says nothing about the consistency of the specific grade you buy.
- Third-party trade-platform "verified supplier" badges evidence a commercial relationship with the platform, not product quality.
- A single excellent pre-shipment sample proves the supplier can make good material once. It does not prove they can repeat it.

### Write a specification that catches drift

Most quality disputes are not about the headline assay. They are about a secondary parameter nobody specified.

For alkaline silicates, the headline number — the SiO₂:Na₂O ratio — is rarely the problem. What causes trouble downstream is iron content, insolubles, moisture and particle size distribution. Iron is a peroxide-decomposition catalyst, so a silicate carrying elevated iron actively undermines a bleaching or cleaning system it was added to support. Insolubles show up as residue and filter loading. Moisture and PSD determine whether a solid grade cakes in storage or doses erratically.

The general rule: **specify the parameter that determines your process outcome, not just the one that names the product.** For each critical parameter, state the limit, the test method, and whether it is reported on the COA. A limit that is never measured is not a limit.

It is also worth stating what happens on drift. If a parameter moves within specification but outside historical range, do you want notification? Many suppliers will agree to this and it converts a surprise into a scheduled conversation.

### What to look at on an audit

An audit that walks the plant and admires the equipment achieves little. The useful questions are narrow:

- **Sampling.** Where in the process is the retained sample taken — from the batch, or from a composite? A composite hides the variability you are trying to detect.
- **Retention.** Are retained samples kept, for how long, and can they be produced on request?
- **Calibration.** Which instruments generate the COA values, and when were they last calibrated against a traceable standard?
- **Out-of-specification handling.** What happens to a failing batch? Reprocessing is normal and acceptable; the answer you want is that it is documented and re-tested, not that it never happens.
- **Change control.** If they change raw-material source or process parameters, are you told? For a bulk commodity, this is where undisclosed drift begins.

Where an on-site audit isn't proportionate to the spend — often the case for commodity inorganics — a structured document review plus a video walk-through of the production and QC areas captures much of the value.

### Design incoming QC around real failure modes

Incoming inspection that repeats the supplier's full COA is expensive and low-yield. Design it instead around what actually goes wrong:

- **Identity and gross error** — a rapid check that the material is what the label says. Cheap, and it catches the rare but severe mix-up.
- **The parameter your process is sensitive to** — for silicates in a peroxide system, that means iron rather than assay.
- **Physical behaviour** — moisture, caking and flow for solid grades, since these fail in storage rather than at goods-in.
- **Trend, not just pass/fail** — plot incoming results over time. Slow drift within specification is the signal that predicts a future problem; a simple pass/fail record hides it entirely.

Reserve full testing for first article, source change, and any lot associated with a process deviation.

### Qualification is a relationship, not a document set

The most reliable predictor of a good supplier is not the paperwork; it is how they behave when something goes wrong. A supplier who calls you before you notice, provides retained samples without argument, and can explain a deviation in process terms is worth more than one with a thicker certificate folder.

That is also why qualification should not be a one-time gate. Re-verify periodically, keep the trend data, and treat any change of production source as a new qualification rather than a continuation of the old one.

Commodity chemicals are only commodities until the moment they interact with your process. Specify for that interaction, verify what you specified, and the "easy" part of the portfolio stays easy.
